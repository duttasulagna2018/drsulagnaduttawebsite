Your picky eater toddler is not starving

Most picky toddlers are not starving. Appetite drops after the first birthday because growth slows down, and fear of new foods is a normal stage. If your child is growing along their line on the growth chart, the plate matters less than you think. The one habit Dr Dutta asks families to fix is too much milk.

Is it normal for a toddler to be a picky eater?

Yes. Picky eating is one of the most common things parents of toddlers worry about, and in most children it passes on its own.

A large Dutch study followed about 4,000 children. Around one in four was a picky eater at 18 months and again at 3 years, but by age 6 this had fallen to about one in eight.1 Nearly half of all the children were picky at some point, and about two thirds of the early picky eaters had grown out of it within three years.1

A smaller group stayed picky for longer. Those children deserve a closer look, as explained further down.

Why did my toddler's appetite suddenly drop after the first birthday?

Because your child is growing more slowly. A baby grows very fast in the first year. In the second year the weight gain slows a lot, and so does hunger.

So the child who finished every bowl of khichdi at nine months may push the plate away at eighteen months. This is how the body is meant to work. It is not a sign of illness, and it is not something you caused.

Toddler appetite also swings from meal to meal. A child may eat well at lunch and almost nothing at dinner, then eat a big breakfast. Look at the whole week, not one meal.

Why does my toddler refuse to even try new foods?

Refusing new foods is called food neophobia (fear of new foods). It is a normal phase in young children, and it is different from being a fussy eater.

Researchers separate the two. Neophobia means avoiding foods the child has not seen before. Picky eating means refusing many foods, including ones the child already knows.2 Both are shaped by the child's age and personality, by what the family does, and by pressure at the table.2

In practice, a new food often needs to sit on the plate many times before your child will touch it. Each calm look, sniff or lick is progress.

How do I know if my picky toddler is eating enough?

Check the growth chart, not the plate. If your child's weight and height are rising steadily along their own curve, they are eating enough for their body.

Lots of parents believe their young child eats badly, yet most of these children have only a mild problem or none at all.3 Other good signs are:

  • Plenty of energy for play.
  • Pale yellow urine several times a day.
  • Soft stools without straining.
  • Normal milestones for their age.

Bring the health card to your paediatrician and ask to see the chart. Seeing the line go up is often the most reassuring thing for a worried family.

Should I force my child to finish the plate?

No. Pressure makes children eat less, not more, and it makes meals unpleasant for everyone.

In one experiment, preschool children were asked to finish their soup on some days and left alone on others. They ate more when nobody pushed them, and they said far fewer negative things about the food.4

Pressure includes "one more bite", bribes with chocolate, threats, and praise only when the plate is empty. It also includes chasing a child round the flat with a bowl. Dr Dutta knows grandmothers do this out of love, often because they grew up when food was scarce. A calm talk in clinic with the whole family often helps more than parents expect.

What is the division of responsibility in feeding?

It is a simple rule from the American dietitian Ellyn Satter. You decide what food is served, when and where. Your child decides whether to eat and how much.

In a small US survey of parents of 2 to 6 year olds, those who followed this approach more closely had children at lower nutrition risk, and used less pressure to eat.5

So you choose that lunch is at one o'clock, at the table, and it is rice, dal, a vegetable and a piece of fish. Your child chooses to eat the rice and leave the fish. You do not cook a second meal, and you do not argue.

Experts describe parent feeding styles as responsive, controlling, indulgent or neglectful.3 This rule is the responsive style in action. It feels uncomfortable at first. Most families report that meals get calmer within a couple of weeks.

Is it okay to feed my toddler in front of cartoons on the phone?

Dr Dutta would stop it. A child who eats while staring at a screen does not notice hunger or fullness, and soon will only eat with the phone on.

Spoon-feeding a distracted child can get more food in today. Over months it removes the child's own control over eating, and it takes away one of the best times of the day to talk together.

Wean it slowly if this is your current routine. Make the screen smaller, then turn the sound off, then move it across the room, then put it away. Expect a few hard days. Children adjust faster than adults.

Does eating together as a family help a fussy eater?

Yes. Children copy what they see, and shared meals are one of the easiest ways to show them how to eat.

A review of 17 studies found that children who ate with their family 3 or more times a week had healthier eating patterns than those who did so less often.6 Their odds of eating healthy foods were about a quarter higher, and their odds of eating unhealthy foods about a fifth lower.6

Your meal does not need to be fancy. Sit together on the floor mat or at the table, eat the same food, switch off the television, and talk. Put a small amount of family food on your child's plate next to one food they usually like.

How many times should I offer a new vegetable before giving up?

Many more times than most parents try. Liking for a food usually grows with repeated tastes, so a single refusal tells you very little.

In a trial with children aged 2 to 6, parents offered a small taste of a disliked vegetable every day for 14 days. Those children liked and ate more of that vegetable afterwards, compared with children whose parents only got advice or nothing at all.7

Keep the taste tiny, a pea-sized piece or one slice of carrot. Do not reward or punish. Just offer, eat some yourself, and move on.

How do I get my toddler to eat fruits and vegetables?

Offer them often, in small amounts, in forms your child can handle, and eat them yourself in front of your child.

  • Mix soft vegetables into foods your child already eats: lauki or carrot in dal, palak in paratha dough, pumpkin in khichdi.
  • Offer soft finger foods: well-steamed beans or carrot, ripe banana, soft papaya cubes, cooked pumpkin pieces.
  • Let your child help wash or pick vegetables at the bazaar.
  • Serve the vegetable first, when your child is hungriest.

Hard raw vegetable sticks, raw fruit chunks, whole nuts and whole grapes can cause choking in young children, so cook, soften or chop them into tiny pieces, and keep your child seated while eating.8

Hiding vegetables is fine as a bonus, but keep serving them in plain view too. The aim is for your child to learn to accept them, not only to swallow them unknowingly.

Should I let my toddler feed himself even if it gets messy?

Yes. Self-feeding gives your child control, and control makes children more willing to eat.

This is the battle of the spoons. Around the first birthday, many children grab the spoon and refuse to be fed. Give them their own spoon and some finger foods while you help with a second spoon. Expect rice on the floor and dal on the face. Put a mat or old newspaper under the chair.

Messy self-feeding also builds hand skills and lets your child stop when full. Clean up after the meal, not during it.

How much food does a toddler actually need at a meal?

Much less than an adult. A toddler's tummy is small, and a portion that looks tiny to you may be a full meal for them.

Many parents judge their child against an adult plate, so the child always seems to "eat nothing". Start with small servings of each food. Your child can always ask for more, and a small plate feels less scary than a heap.

Keep set times for meals and planned snacks, with only water in between. A child who nibbles biscuits all afternoon will not be hungry at dinner. When your child stops eating or starts playing, calmly end the meal.

How much milk should a toddler drink a day?

Less than most Indian toddlers drink. European experts advise keeping cow's milk under 500 ml a day in toddlers, and not using it as the main drink before 12 months of age.9

Count all milk in that total, including milk in porridge and tea. Some practical steps:

  • Offer milk from a cup, not a bottle, after the first birthday.
  • Give milk after a meal or with a snack, not instead of a meal.
  • Skip the sugar and the malted powders.
  • Stop night bottles. They fill the child up and harm the teeth.

Can drinking too much milk make my toddler eat less food?

Yes. Milk is filling, so a toddler who drinks large amounts has little room for food. It can also lead to iron deficiency.

Cow's milk contains very little iron. Its calcium and casein (a milk protein) also block iron from other foods being absorbed.10 In babies under one year, cow's milk can cause hidden blood loss from the gut.10 High milk intake is a known risk factor for iron deficiency anaemia in toddlers.9

Dr Dutta sees this pattern most weeks: a child who "only drinks milk" because they will not eat, and will not eat because of the milk. Cutting milk back is often the change that helps most.

Should I give my picky eater an appetite tonic or health drink?

Usually not. Appetite syrups and malted health drinks do not fix picky eating, and the drinks often add sugar and fill the child up further.

A child who is growing normally does not need them. A child who is not growing needs a proper check, not a tonic. If your doctor finds a real deficiency, such as low iron, it will be treated directly.

When is picky eating a feeding disorder?

When the child is not growing well, the list of accepted foods keeps shrinking, or eating causes fear, gagging or distress.

Doctors group feeding problems into three kinds: limited appetite, selective eating, and fear of feeding. Each runs from normal to severe, and only a small share of children have a serious disorder.3 Causes include reflux, constipation, cow's milk protein allergy, sensory problems with textures, and conditions such as autism.

A rare but serious form is called ARFID (avoidant restrictive food intake disorder), where the child eats very few foods and weight or nutrition suffers.

When should I take my picky eater to the doctor?

See your paediatrician if any of these apply:

  • Your child is losing weight, or is dropping down across the lines on the growth chart.
  • Your child accepts very few foods, or is dropping foods they used to eat.
  • Your child often gags, chokes or vomits during meals, or is frightened of eating.
  • Your child looks pale, tires easily, or eats mud or chalk.
  • There is long-lasting constipation, diarrhoea or tummy pain.
  • You also have worries about speech, play or behaviour.
  • Mealtimes have become a daily fight and you need a plan.
